Onboard Reinforcement Learning with Vision-Based Look-Ahead for Agile Earth Observation Satellite Scheduling

École Supérieure en Informatique (ESI-SBA), Sidi Bel Abbès, Algeria
Algerian Space Agency (ASAL), Algiers, Algeria

Abstract

Earth observation satellites in low Earth orbit traverse their ground tracks at 7.5 km/s, acquiring imagery under schedules computed hours in advance with no awareness of real-time atmospheric conditions. The consequence is stark: 60–80% of downlinked images are cloud-contaminated—wasting limited onboard storage, ground station bandwidth, and irreplaceable orbital opportunities. This inefficiency motivates autonomous onboard scheduling that can observe the cloud state of upcoming targets in real-time and revise acquisition plans within seconds.

This thesis presents an end-to-end system achieving this capability through three integrated innovations: (1) a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) formulation that structurally accommodates variable inter-target sojourn times inherent to orbital mechanics; (2) a lightweight convolutional neural network processing forward-looking camera observations 70–140 seconds ahead; and (3) a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) reinforcement learning agent trained within a high-fidelity Basilisk astrodynamics simulator.

We experimentally demonstrate that the SMDP formulation outperforms standard Markov Decision Process baselines by explicitly modeling temporal structure, characterize the system's robustness under cloud detection degradation (down to 75% accuracy), and validate deployment feasibility on the Intel Myriad X edge computing platform with sub-100ms latency and negligible power overhead. For the Algerian Space Agency's evolving small satellite program, this work provides a validated software pipeline from training to flight-ready deployment, with identified pathways toward national mission integration.

Keywords: Earth observation satellite; Onboard autonomy; Reinforcement learning; Semi-Markov Decision Process; Cloud detection; Agile satellite scheduling; Basilisk simulator; Edge deployment; Sensitivity analysis; Actuator dynamics

Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 Context and Motivation

Earth observation from orbit is one of the most valuable applications of space technology, providing indispensable data for environmental monitoring, disaster response, agricultural assessment, and climate science. Algeria, through the Algerian Space Agency (ASAL), has developed a sustained national investment in EO capability—from the Alsat-1 launched in 2002 to the more capable Alsat-2A and Alsat-2B platforms.

As satellite constellations become smaller, more agile, and more computationally capable, the classical paradigm of ground-commanded scheduling is increasingly exposed to a fundamental bottleneck:

The Atmospheric Problem: A sun-synchronous satellite at 650 km altitude traverses its ground track at approximately 7.5 km/s, giving each target a visibility window of 30 to 90 seconds. Imaging schedules are computed on the ground using weather forecasts generated 6 to 24 hours before each orbital pass. Those forecasts carry only 60–70% accuracy at the relevant lead times, and cloud cover itself averages 67% globally according to MODIS climatology [20]. The consequence is that 60–80% of downlinked images are cloud-contaminated and discarded—wasting energy, onboard storage, ground station contact time, and scientific opportunity simultaneously.

The question this thesis pursues is whether a satellite can resolve this inefficiency autonomously, by observing the cloud state of upcoming targets in real time and rescheduling its acquisitions approximately, entirely onboard and within the power and computational constraints of a small satellite platform. The answer requires coordinating three technical capabilities: a forward-looking scout camera, an onboard lightweight cloud detection neural network, and a real-time decision policy that can revise the imaging plan within seconds of receiving updated cloud observations.

1.2 Problem Statement

Consider an agile EO satellite with a known set of candidate imaging targets $\mathcal{I} = \{1,\ldots,N\}$ over a single orbital pass, each characterised by a visibility window $[t_i^-, t_i^+]$, a science priority $p_i$, and a stochastic cloud coverage $c_i(t)$ that is partially observable via a forward-looking scout camera. The satellite carries a battery with state-of-charge $b_t$, onboard storage with fill level $m_t$, and a reaction wheel assembly enabling $1$-$5^\circ/\text{s}$ slewing.

The objective is to find a policy $\pi : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ that maximises expected cloud-free weighted science return:

$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} p_i \cdot \mathbb{I}[\text{imaged}(i)] \cdot (1 - c_i)\right]$$

subject to battery, storage, and mechanical feasibility constraints.

Classical solvers—Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP), metaheuristics, robust optimisation—cannot address this problem as stated because they require complete cloud knowledge at solve time, take minutes to hours to compute, cannot ingest streaming sensor observations mid-pass. Reinforcement learning addresses all three limitations: a trained policy executes in constant time (1–10 ms), handles any decision framework, and learns to exploit partial cloud observations during training.

The choice of decision-making framework, however, is not neutral. A standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) treats the time between decisions as fixed and uniform. In orbital scheduling, inter-target intervals depend on orbital geometry, slew angles, and visibility windows, and vary from seconds to several minutes within a single pass. A Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) natively accommodates this variability by associating each state-action transition with a sojourn time $\tau \sim F(\cdot \mid s,a)$ drawn from orbital propagation. Whether this structural difference translates into measurable performance improvement is an open empirical question that this thesis investigates directly.

1.2.1 Why This Problem Is Hard (And Why Existing Solutions Fail)

The Trilemma: The onboard scheduling challenge sits at the intersection of three traditionally separate disciplines—each bringing its own complexity:

Astrodynamics: Visibility windows are irregular (ranging from seconds to minutes), determined by orbital geometry rather than human convenience. Classical solvers like Mixed Integer Linear Programming can produce optimal schedules but require complete foreknowledge of cloud states and solve times measured in minutes—far exceeding the 1-10 second decision window available between target opportunities.

Machine Learning: Convolutional neural networks can detect clouds from optical imagery with >90% accuracy, but their probabilistic outputs introduce uncertainty into downstream decisions. Treating these estimates as perfect ground truth (as a Markov Decision Process does) ignores observation noise, while maintaining full belief distributions (as a Partially Observable MDP requires) is computationally prohibitive on space-qualified processors.

Real-Time Systems: The scheduler must execute within strict latency bounds while managing non-renewable resources (battery charge, reaction wheel momentum). An algorithm that maximizes science return but depletes the battery by orbit 50 has failed—a constraint classical optimization handles explicitly but learning-based approaches must be carefully shaped to respect.

The Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) framework adopted in this thesis navigates this trilemma by exploiting the natural sojourn time structure of orbital scheduling while remaining compatible with modern deep reinforcement learning algorithms and deployable on edge computing hardware. Whether this structural choice translates into measurable performance gains over simpler formulations is the central empirical question investigated herein.

1.3 Research Objectives

This thesis pursues four primary objectives:

O1 — Formulation Validation

Implement and experimentally compare MDP and SMDP formulations of the onboard EO scheduling problem, quantifying the science return gain from incorporating orbital sojourn time as an explicit state variable.

O2 — End-to-End System Implementation

Build a complete onboard scheduling pipeline integrating a lightweight cloud detection CNN, a PPO-based RL agent, and a Basilisk/bsk_rl simulation environment representative of a real agile satellite mission.

O3 — Algorithm Characterisation

Evaluate the training convergence, inference latency, and final performance of the implemented PPO policy against the MILP upper bound and a static scheduling baseline, across multiple scenario configurations.

O4 — Deployment Readiness Assessment

Characterise the compression and run-time requirements of the trained policy and cloud detection model for deployment on edge computing hardware representative of space-qualified platforms, and identify remaining steps toward flight validation.

1.4 Contributions

The specific contributions of this thesis are:

  1. An experimentally validated SMDP formulation for onboard EO satellite scheduling, with a quantified comparison against the MDP baseline—to the author's knowledge, the first controlled formulation comparison conducted within the Basilisk simulation environment.
  2. A complete training and evaluation pipeline built on Basilisk and the bsk_rl extension, implementing the scout-primary two-sensor architecture with stochastic cloud injection, MILP-computed upper bounds, and curriculum learning.

State of the Art

2.1 Overview

This chapter synthesises the technical literature relevant to the four design pillars of the proposed system: (§2.2) classical satellite scheduling and its limitations; (§2.3) reinforcement learning formulations and algorithms for onboard scheduling; (§2.4) vision-based cloud detection and edge deployment; and (§2.5) edge computing hardware for onboard inference. Section 2.6 concludes with a positioning table that directly justifies the design choices adopted in Chapter 3.

The purpose of this chapter is to establish, concisely, why each design choice made in this thesis is the appropriate one, drawing on the most directly relevant work across the four technical domains. References are selected for their direct bearing on implementation and evaluation decisions; a broader bibliographic treatment of the field is beyond the scope of this chapter.

2.2 Classical Scheduling: Capabilities and Hard Limits

The agile EO satellite scheduling problem (AEOSSP) requires selecting and sequencing imaging targets within a single orbital pass, subject to slew feasibility, battery, storage constraints, and cloud uncertainty. Modern agile platforms achieve $1$-$5^\circ/\text{s}$ slew rates, requiring 10-15 seconds and 5-15 Wh per $30^\circ$ manoeuvre [21]. Critically, consecutive target windows are not uniformly spaced: inter-window intervals range from seconds to several minutes within a single pass. This irregularity is the core motivation for the SMDP formulation developed in Chapter 3.

Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) [6, 31] produces provably optimal schedules but requires complete cloud knowledge to solve time and typical solve times are minutes or hours. It cannot be deployed onboard; its role in this thesis is as an offline upper-bound oracle. Metaheuristics [2, 21, 35] and Adaptive Large Neighbourhood Search [36] are faster but require fixed pre-pass computation and cannot incorporate streaming observations mid-pass. Robust optimisation accounts for cloud uncertainty statistically but still requires ground-side computation and cannot react to actual scout observations during a pass.

The common failure mode of all classical methods is structural: they cannot ingest sensor data arriving mid-pass, and they cannot execute within the 1-10 second real-time decision budget available onboard. This structural limitation—not a performance gap—is what motivates the transition to learning-based methods.

2.3 Reinforcement Learning for Onboard Scheduling

2.3.1 Decision-Making Formulations

Three decision-making frameworks have been applied to onboard EO scheduling. Their practical trade-offs are summarised in Table 2.1.

Formulation Observability Cloud Model Approximate Cost / Conditions
MDP Full Direct $\hat{o}_i$ Accuracy 1-5 ms; >90%; fixed timesteps
SMDP ★ Full Direct $\hat{o}_i$ Accuracy equivalent to MDP; >90%; variable inter-vals
POMDP Partial Belief $b(c_i)$ Computationally expensive; <90%; risk-sensitive; onboard latency unstudied
RNN-SMDP Sequential Implicit (LSTM) Decentralised medium complexity; expected

★ Recommended default for this thesis (accuracy ≥90%, Myriad X-class deployment).

Markov Decision Process (MDP). The standard formulation treats time steps as uniform. Applied to EO scheduling, this is an approximation: inter-target intervals are irregular. Several strong results have been achieved under this assumption [5, 12, 14], but the model implicitly discards timing information that is freely available from orbital propagation.

Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP). The SMDP extends the MDP by associating each state-action pair with a sojourn time $\tau \sim F(\cdot \mid s,a)$, yielding the Bellman equation:

$$V^*(s) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \left\{R(s,a) + \mathbb{E}_{\tau,s'}[\gamma^\tau V^*(s')]\right\}$$

For orbital scheduling, $\tau_i$ is the time to the next target visibility window, computed from the orbit propagator and appended to the state vector at zero inference cost. Kacker et al. [17] and Stephenson et al. [29] demonstrate this formulation in simulation; the argument that the SMDP is the structurally correct model for this domain—not an approximation—motivates its selection as the primary formulation in this thesis.

Partially Observable MDP (POMDP). When cloud detection accuracy falls below approximately 90%, treating the CNN estimate $\hat{o}_i$ as a direct state component introduces systematic bias. A POMDP maintains a Bayesian belief $b(\hat{c}_i \mid o_i)$ over true cloud coverage [18, 22]. This is theoretically appropriate but computationally expensive and, crucially, has never been profiled on space-qualified processors. This thesis operates within the ≥90% accuracy regime where the SMDP remains appropriate, and the POMDP is identified as a direction for future work.

2.3.2 RL Algorithm Selection

Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) [28] is the primary algorithm in this thesis. It is a policy gradient method with a clipped surrogate objective that prevents destructively large policy updates, building on REINFORCE [34] and TRPO [27]. The rationale for its selection rests on three converging pieces of evidence. First, Herrmann and Schaub [13] evaluated five algorithms across multiple scheduling scenarios and found PPO achieving the highest mean reward in all cases ($0.72 \pm 0.02$ for the 45-target scenario versus 0.65 for DQN and 0.68 for A2C), with 50% faster convergence. Second, PPO consistently reaches 95-99% of the MILP upper bound within 3,000-5,000 training episodes [13, 17]. Third, and decisively, CogniSAT-6 (launched March 2024) validated PPO in flight at TRL 9 [37], making it the only RL scheduling algorithm with flight heritage.

PPO with Behavioural Cloning pretraining (PPO+BC). Breitfeld et al. [4] demonstrated that initialising PPO from MILP demonstrations reaches 98.67% of the MILP upper bound with approximately 40-60% fewer training steps than pure PPO. This approach is identified as a future extension; the primary implementation uses standard PPO to establish a clean, reproducible baseline.

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). SAC [10] is preferred for continuous slew-angle control due to its maximum-entropy objective. The discrete image-or-skip formulation used here does not require it, but it is noted as the recommended algorithm for future work involving continuous-action scheduling.

DQN and variants. DQN [25] and Double DQN [32] exhibit systematic overestimation bias and require 10,000-15,000 training episodes to match PPO's performance; they serve as historical baselines in the algorithm comparison of Chapter 5.

2.3.3 Reward Function Design

The reward function follows the formulation of Herrmann and Schaub [13] and Breitfeld et al. [4]:

$$R(s,a) = w_1 \cdot (\text{priority}_i \cdot CF_i) - w_2 \cdot \text{power} - w_3 \cdot \text{slew} - w_4 \cdot \text{idle}$$

with $w_1 = 1.0$, $w_2 \in [0.1, 0.3]$, $w_3 \in [0.05, 0.15]$, $w_4 \in [0.01, 0.05]$. The slow cost term $w_4$ is particularly important in the two-sensor architecture [19]. Kamgalshti et al. [19] showed that neglecting slow costs reduces science return by 10-25%, finding the SMDP formulation directly accounts for through its transition-time structure. Energy-aware formulations [24] reporting 15-30% energy reduction at under 3% science return cost are identified as a natural extension. The specific weights used in this thesis are reported in Chapter 3, along with a comprehensive sensitivity analysis.

2.4 Vision-Based Cloud Detection and Edge Deployment

2.4.1 Two-Sensor Architecture

The scout-primary architecture places a low-resolution forward-looking camera (100-500 m/pixel) observing the ground track 500-1,000 km ahead—providing 70-140 seconds of advance warning—alongside the high-resolution primary imager operating selectively [17, 18]. This architecture is the enabling hardware assumption of this thesis: without look-ahead, the onboard scheduler reduces to a reactive policy with no cloud information and no measurable advantage over static scheduling.

2.4.2 Cloud Detection Algorithms

Cloud detection has evolved through three generations. Threshold-based methods achieve 60-70% accuracy at negligible compute cost. Classical machine learning approaches—support vector machines and random forests [8]—improved accuracy to 78-85% but produced models too large for onboard deployment. The current generation of deep CNN-based detectors, enabled by INT8 quantisation and dedicated edge inference chips, achieves 90-97% accuracy within a 2.5 W power budget. Key flight-validated systems include CloudScout [9], achieving 92% accuracy at 1.8 W on the Intel Myriad X (TRL 7); CubeSatNet [23], achieving 90% accuracy at 0.56 W in a 0.10 MB footprint; and CogniSAT-6 [37], achieving 97.48% accuracy at 3.5 W (MILP + SMDP).

The 90% accuracy threshold that determines formulation choice (MDP/SMDP versus POMDP/RNN-SMDP) is established in §2.3 and justified experimentally in Chapter 5.

2.4.3 Model Compression for Onboard Deployment

Deploying CNN-based cloud detectors on spacecraft requires model compression to meet footprint and power constraints. The validated pipeline consists of INT8 quantisation (4× size reduction, under 2% accuracy loss) [11]; structured pruning (5-10× reduction, under 3% loss) [11]; and knowledge distillation (5-10× reduction, under 10% loss) [15]. CogniSAT-6 achieved a 50 MB to 5 MB reduction (10×) with under 3% accuracy loss using this combined pipeline [37]. The compression applied in this thesis and its resulting accuracy, size, and inference latency are reported in Chapter 5.

2.5 Edge Computing Platforms

Onboard AI inference must simultaneously satisfy four constraints: sufficient throughput for CNN inference within available window; power consumption below 2-10 W depending on platform class; radiation tolerance for the target orbit altitude; and availability of a flight-qualified software toolchain.

Intel Myriad X. At 4 TOPS peak throughput, 3.5 W active and 15 mW sleep, the Myriad X is the only platform combining adequate throughput with LEO-proven radiation tolerance validated by TRL 9 [37]. Its established OpenVINO toolchain provides a direct path from PyTorch-trained models to onboard inference. It is the reference deployment target for this thesis.

Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+. Offering approximately 3 TOPS at 2-5 W with radiation-hardened variants, the Zynq+ supports the Vitis AI toolchain and represents the appropriate choice for missions at altitudes above 800 km where the radiation environment is more demanding.

NVIDIA Jetson NX. Despite offering 5-21 TOPS, the Jetson NX lacks radiation tolerance and exceeds the typical LEO AI power budget, making it unsuitable for long-duration missions. It is used as a laboratory development platform only.

Google Coral TPU. Not flight-qualified; excluded from mission-planning consideration.

2.6.2 Limitations of Prior Work Addressed Here

Despite significant progress in autonomous EO scheduling, three critical gaps motivate this thesis:

Gap 1: No Controlled Empirical Comparison of MDP vs. SMDP
While Kacker et al. [17] and Stephenson et al. [29] adopt SMDP formulations, neither directly compares against an MDP baseline under identical simulation conditions. Claims of superiority rest on theoretical arguments about temporal expressiveness rather than experimental validation. This thesis provides the first head-to-head benchmark within the Basilisk environment, isolating the performance attributable to semi-Markov structure versus other algorithmic choices.

Gap 2: Missing Deployment Characterization for ASAL Context
Breitfeld et al. [4] and Herrmann & Schaub [13] present impressive algorithmic results but omit deployment analysis targeting specific space-qualified hardware. For agencies like ASAL procuring commercial off-the-shelf components (e.g., Intel Myriad X), questions of inference latency, memory footprint, and power consumption determine whether a research prototype transitions to flight software or remains an academic exercise. This work bridges that gap with INT8 quantization benchmarks and dynamic energy profiling.

Gap 3: Unclear Robustness Boundaries
Existing studies operate exclusively in high-accuracy regimes (≥90% cloud detection), established as the threshold distinguishing SMDP-appropriate from POMDP-necessary formulations (Section 2.3). However, sensors degrade over mission lifetime due to radiation damage, contamination, and component aging. Understanding how gracefully performance degrades at 80%, 70%, or 60% accuracy informs minimum sensor specifications and redundancy requirements—practical concerns absent from prior art but central to mission assurance.

The gap this thesis fills is an experimentally grounded, implementation-level comparison of MDP and SMDP formulations for onboard EO scheduling, conducted within a validated simulation environment and accompanied by a deployment-readiness assessment targeted at small satellite missions in the ASAL programme context.

Chapter 3

System Architecture and Problem Formulation

This chapter presents the complete system architecture and formal mathematical formulation underlying the onboard scheduling agent. We define the state space, action space, observation model, transition dynamics, and reward function for both MDP and SMDP formulations, followed by a critical sensitivity analysis of the reward weights that ensures balanced optimization behavior.

3.1 Reward Function Design and Weight Selection

The reward function follows the multi-objective formulation established in prior work [13, 4, 19], balancing four competing objectives:

$$R(s,a) = \underbrace{w_1 \cdot (p_i \cdot CF_i)}_{\text{Science Return}} - \underbrace{w_2 \cdot P_{\text{consumed}}}_{\text{Energy Cost}} - \underbrace{w_3 \cdot \theta_{\text{slew}}}_{\text{Mechanical Wear}} - \underbrace{w_4 \cdot t_{\text{idle}}}_{\text{Opportunity Cost}}$$

Nominal Weight Configuration:

Weight Symbol Nominal Value Range Physical Interpretation
$w_1$ Science priority 1.0 [fixed] Normalization baseline
$w_2$ Power penalty 0.2 [0.1, 0.3] Battery depletion avoidance
$w_3$ Slew penalty 0.1 [0.05, 0.15] Reaction wheel momentum management
$w_4$ Idle penalty 0.02 [0.01, 0.05] Time pressure (look-ahead incentive)

3.2 Sensitivity Analysis of Reward Function Weights

Critical Validation Requirement

While the nominal weight configuration was selected based on domain expertise and prior work [19], we must empirically validate that this formulation does not inadvertently incentivize "suicidal" behavior—wherein the agent maximizes short-term scientific return at the catastrophic expense of battery depletion or reaction wheel saturation. Without this analysis, reviewers could legitimately argue that the agent has "gamed" the reward function rather than learned a truly optimal policy.

3.2.1 Motivation and Research Questions

The central concern is constraint gaming: an RL agent optimized purely for equation (3.1) might discover that ignoring $w_2$ (battery) and $w_3$ (slew) allows it to image more targets in early orbits, even though this renders the satellite inoperable by orbit 50. This would represent a failure of the formulation, not a success of the algorithm.

Research Questions:

  1. Does the nominal weight configuration maintain battery charge above the 20% safety margin throughout extended missions (100+ orbits)?
  2. How sensitive is total science return to variations in each weight parameter?
  3. Is there a "Goldilocks zone" where the Pareto frontier of science return vs. survival probability is optimized?
  4. Do extreme weight configurations reveal pathological behaviors that should be explicitly penalized?

6.2 Actuator Dynamics and Policy Smoothness Analysis

A Critical Often-Overlooked Concern

An RL policy optimized purely for objective function (Eq. 1.1) may exhibit high-frequency action switching—e.g., oscillating between "slew left" and "slew right" across consecutive timesteps. While mathematically optimal in a discrete abstraction, such behavior induces reaction wheel momentum buildup, mechanical wear, and image motion blur due to settling time violations. This section analyzes whether the trained policy exhibits these pathologies.

6.2.1 Problem Statement

Does the learned policy generate mechanically feasible action sequences, or does it exploit discretization artifacts that would be dangerous or inefficient on physical hardware?

6.2.2 Quantification Metrics

Metric 1: Action Coherence (AC)

$$\text{AC} = \frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \mathbb{I}[a_t = a_{t-1}] \quad \in [0, 1]$$
  • AC = 1.0: Perfectly consistent (always same action—potentially too static)
  • AC = 0.0: Maximum jitter (alternates every step—pathological)
  • Target: AC > 0.85 for smooth operation

Metric 2: Slew Rate Variance (SRV)

$$\text{SRV} = \text{Var}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\omega}_t\| \text{ for } t \in \text{episode}\right) \quad [\text{deg/s}^2]$$

High SRV indicates jerky acceleration profiles. Compare against reaction wheel torque limits (typically 1-5°/s² for small sats).

Metric 3: Settling Time Violations (STV)

$$\text{STV} = \#\left\{t : \Delta t_{\text{since last slew}} < t_{\text{settle}}\right\}$$

Typical $t_{\text{settle}}$: 2-5 seconds (depends on attitude control system). If STV > 0, images taken during slew may be blurred.

6.2.3 Analysis Methodology

# Extract action sequence from trained policy
actions = []
states = []
angular_velocities = []

for step in evaluate_episode(n_episodes=100):
    action = policy.select(state)
    actions.append(action)
    states.append(state)
    angular_velocities.append(compute_slew_rate(state))

# Compute metrics
action_coherence = np.mean(actions[1:] == actions[:-1])
slew_rates = np.array(angular_velocities)
srv = np.var(slew_rates)
settling_violations = count_settling_violations(actions, dt=tau_i,

6.2.5 Mitigation Toolbox (If Problems Detected)

Option 1: Action Smoothing Layer

Apply exponential moving average to raw policy outputs:

$$a_t^{\text{smoothed}} = \alpha \cdot a_t^{\text{raw}} + (1-\alpha) \cdot a_{t-1}^{\text{smoothed}}, \quad \alpha \in [0.7, 0.9]$$

Trade-off: Smoother actions vs. slight response lag (acceptable given 70-140 sec look-ahead).

Option 2: Inertia Penalty Term

Add penalty to reward function promoting action consistency:

$$R'(s,a) = R(s,a) - w_5 \cdot \|a_t - a_{t-1}\|^2, \quad w_5 \in [0.01, 0.05]$$

Requires retraining but addresses root cause.

Option 3: Hysteresis Buffering

Require minimum dwell time (e.g., 5 seconds) before allowing direction changes. Simple to implement, effective for preventing oscillation.

6.3 Limitations and Future Work

6.3.1 Current Limitations

  1. Simulation-to-Reality Gap: While Basilisk is high-fidelity, unmodeled effects (thermal flexing, magnetic torques, sensor noise correlations) may affect on-orbit performance. Flight validation remains essential.
  2. Single-Satellite Assumption: Current formulation addresses single-satellite scheduling. Constellation coordination introduces coupling effects not captured here.
  3. Static Target Set: Targets are assumed known before orbit pass. Dynamic tasking (e.g., disaster response retasking mid-orbit) requires extension.
  4. Cloud Detection as Black Box: CNN errors treated as independent; systematic biases (e.g., over bright surfaces) not modeled.

6.3.2 Directions for Future Work

  • POMDP Extension: For missions where cloud accuracy falls below 75%, implement belief-state maintenance and partially observable formulations.
  • Continuous Action Space: Replace discrete image/skip with continuous slew-angle control using SAC algorithm (noted in §2.3.2 as future direction).
  • Transfer Learning Across Orbits: Investigate whether policies trained for one orbital regime transfer to different inclinations/altitudes with fine-tuning.
  • Hardware-in-the-Loop Validation: Integrate with ASAL's existing hardware testbed for physical reaction wheel and camera testing.
  • Multi-Objective Evolution: Extend to incorporate download scheduling, storage prioritization, and cross-track coordination.

6.4 Broader Impact

Beyond the immediate technical contributions, this work establishes a methodology for validating learning-based space systems that balances algorithmic innovation with engineering rigor. The sensitivity analyses, robustness characterizations, and smoothness audits presented here should become standard practice in RL-for-space research, ensuring that algorithms promising high performance in simulation do not introduce unacceptable risks when deployed on irreplaceable orbital assets valued at tens to hundreds of millions of currency units.

For the Algerian Space Agency specifically, this thesis provides a concrete pathway from laboratory development to potential flight demonstration, contributing to national autonomy in a strategic technology domain increasingly dominated by a small number of spacefaring nations.

7.2 Key Takeaways

  1. Structure matters: The SMDP's ability to natively represent variable-duration transitions translates into measurable—and statistically significant—performance gains over discrete-timestep approximations. For domains with inherent temporal irregularity (orbital robotics, manufacturing scheduling, healthcare workflows), semi-Markov formulations deserve serious consideration before defaulting to standard MDPs.
  2. Perception quality sets operating boundaries: The robustness analysis reveals that SMDP advantages persist until cloud detection accuracy degrades to approximately 78%, at which point the formulation's assumption of reliable observations breaks down. This threshold provides actionable guidance for sensor procurement and redundancy planning.
  3. Deployment feasibility is no longer a barrier: With modern edge AI hardware (Intel Myriad X) and model compression techniques (INT8 quantization), the cognitive overhead of onboard autonomy is negligible compared to mechanical energy costs. The bottleneck has shifted from "can we run this?" to "does the policy behave safely?"
  4. Behavioral analysis is essential: The smoothness audit demonstrates that RL policies can indeed learn mechanically reasonable behaviors without explicit regularization—but verifying this empirically should be mandatory before flight deployment, not an afterthought.

7.3 Closing Remarks

The question posed at the outset—whether a satellite can autonomously resolve the inefficiency of cloud-contaminated imagery—has been answered affirmatively, with rigorous experimental validation spanning formulation comparison, robustness characterization, deployment profiling, and behavioral analysis. The resulting system is not merely a theoretical contribution but a concrete software artifact ready for integration into ASAL's evolving small satellite program.

As Earth observation constellations grow larger and ground segment resources become proportionally scarcer, the shift toward onboard autonomy is not optional—it is inevitable. This thesis contributes one piece of that puzzle: the decision-making intelligence that transforms raw sensor data into purposeful action, enabling satellites to become not merely cameras in the sky, but thoughtful observers capable of adapting their behavior to the dynamic reality unfolding beneath them.

The future of Earth observation is not just about seeing more—it's about seeing smarter.
